🗺️ Where We Are

Lecture 3 ended by separating two kinds of time-series work.

  • Structural: theory with optimising agents, deep parameters with economic meaning (an Euler equation estimated by GMM).
  • Non-structural: models built to describe the data and to forecast it (AR-ARCH-M).

Today we take the non-structural side seriously and ask two questions. What feature of the series do we model? And among the many models that could describe it, how do we choose one?

❓ The Question

Three people, one series

A central-bank economist, a risk manager and a bond trader all watch the monthly change in the US unemployment rate.

  • The economist wants the expected change next month.
  • The risk manager wants to know how large a jump is possible: a high quantile.
  • The trader wants the probability that the rate rises at all.

Same data, three different targets. Each is a feature of one object: the distribution of \(x_{T+1}\) given the past.

📐 The Setting

We observe \(\{x_t\}_{t=1}^{T}\), assumed strictly stationary and ergodic. Non-structural modelling has two aims:

  1. describe the stylised facts of the data;
  2. predict: the one-step forecast \(\hat x_{T+1|T}\) and perhaps multi-step forecasts \(\hat x_{T+h|T}\), \(h > 1\).

Both require the dynamics of \(x_t\) as a function of its past, \[I_{t-1} = \{x_{t-1}, x_{t-2}, \dots\}, \qquad E_{t-1}[\cdot] = E[\,\cdot \mid I_{t-1}].\]

The feature of the conditional distribution we choose to describe is the object of modelling.

📐 Object 1: The Conditional Mean

\[\mu_t = E_{t-1}[x_t] = E[x_t \mid x_{t-1}, x_{t-2}, \dots]\]

Why the mean

Among all predictors \(g\) built from \(I_{t-1}\), \(\mu_t\) minimises the mean squared prediction error (MSPE), \(E[(x_t - g)^2]\).

The natural object for GNP, unemployment, the real exchange rate: series where the level of the next value is what matters.

✏️ Proof: the Mean Minimises MSPE

Take any \(g \in I_{t-1}\) and add and subtract \(\mu_t\): \[E[(x_t - g)^2] = E[(x_t - \mu_t)^2] + 2E[(x_t - \mu_t)(\mu_t - g)] + E[(\mu_t - g)^2].\]

The cross term vanishes by the law of iterated expectations, because \(\mu_t - g\) is known at \(t-1\): \[E[(x_t - \mu_t)(\mu_t - g)] = E\big[(\mu_t - g)\,E_{t-1}[x_t - \mu_t]\big] = E[(\mu_t - g)\cdot 0] = 0.\]

So \(E[(x_t - g)^2] = E[(x_t - \mu_t)^2] + E[(\mu_t - g)^2] \ge E[(x_t - \mu_t)^2]\), with equality only when \(g = \mu_t\). \(\blacksquare\)

📐 Variance, Median, Quantile

  • Conditional variance \(\sigma_t^2 = \operatorname{Var}_{t-1}[x_t] = E_{t-1}[(x_t - \mu_t)^2]\): volatility, the object for asset returns.
  • Conditional median: optimal under absolute loss (minimum mean absolute prediction error). It is robust to outliers, and it exists even when thick tails leave the mean undefined.
  • Conditional quantile \(q_t^{\alpha}\), defined by \(\Pr_{t-1}\{x_t \le q_t^{\alpha}\} = \alpha\). A low quantile of returns is the Value-at-Risk; \(\alpha = 0.5\) gives the median.

Each is the optimal predictor under its own loss function.

✏️ Why the Median, Why the Quantile

Absolute loss. For a continuous \(x\) with distribution function \(F\), \[\frac{d}{dm}E|x - m| = \Pr(x < m) - \Pr(x > m) = 2F(m) - 1,\] which is zero at \(F(m) = \tfrac12\): the median.

Check loss. Replace \(|u|\) by \(\rho_\alpha(u) = u\,(\alpha - \mathbf 1\{u < 0\})\), which charges \(\alpha\) per unit of under-prediction and \(1-\alpha\) per unit of over-prediction. Then \[\frac{d}{dm}E\,\rho_\alpha(x - m) = F(m) - \alpha = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; m = q^\alpha.\] Conditioning on \(I_{t-1}\) throughout gives \(q_t^\alpha\).

The check-loss argument is not in the 2016 notes: added from Koenker (2005), Quantile Regression, ch. 1.

📐 Moments, Probability, Density

  • Higher moments: \(S_t = E_{t-1}[(x_t - \mu_t)^3]\) measures asymmetry and \(K_t = E_{t-1}[(x_t - \mu_t)^4]\) tail thickness. Skewness and kurtosis proper are \(S_t/\sigma_t^3\) and \(K_t/\sigma_t^4\).
  • Conditional probability \(p_t = \Pr_{t-1}\{x_t > c\}\): will the rate rise, will the firm default.
  • Conditional density \(f_{t-1}(x_t)\): contains every object above, and is the hardest to specify and identify.

Direct modelling specifies a model for the object of interest itself (a quantile regression for \(q_t^\alpha\)). Indirect modelling specifies something richer, typically the whole conditional density, and derives the object from it.

💻 Same Mean, Moving Quantiles

AR(1)-ARCH(1): the mean barely moves, the 5% quantile does 
n <- 400; r <- e <- s2 <- numeric(n); s2[1] <- 1
for (t in 2:n) {
  s2[t] <- 0.2 + 0.7 * e[t - 1]^2            # conditional variance
  e[t]  <- sqrt(s2[t]) * rnorm(1)
  r[t]  <- 0.1 * r[t - 1] + e[t]             # conditional mean 0.1 r_{t-1}
}
d <- data.frame(t = 1:n, r = r, mu = c(0, 0.1 * r[-n]), s = sqrt(s2))
ggplot(d, aes(t)) + geom_line(aes(y = r), colour = "grey55") +
  geom_line(aes(y = mu), colour = "#2f4b7c", linewidth = 1) +
  geom_line(aes(y = mu - 1.645 * s), colour = "#8b2635", linewidth = 0.8) +
  labs(x = "t", y = expression(x[t]))

Blue: \(\mu_t\). Red: the conditional 5% quantile \(\mu_t - 1.645\,\sigma_t\). A mean model would call this series nearly unpredictable; a quantile model would not.

🧭 Model Selection: the Principle

Selection runs object → class of models → specific model.

Object Class Specific models
\(\mu_t\) AR AR(1), AR(2), …
\(\mu_t\) TAR (threshold AR) TAR(1,1,1), …
\(\mu_t\) MSW (Markov switching) two-regime AR, …

Workflow: (1) specify the object, (2) collect the data, (3) choose the model, (4) select within and across classes. Step 4 is the hard one. The model is an approximation to the data-generating process (DGP), not the DGP itself.

🔧 Tool 1: Diagnostic Testing

Does the estimated model conform to its own assumptions? Test, on the residuals:

  • no serial correlation (Ljung–Box, Breusch–Godfrey);
  • no conditional heteroskedasticity;
  • normality, if it was assumed (Jarque–Bera);
  • parameter stability (Andrews’ sup-type test);
  • a correct distributional assumption: the probability integral transforms \(u_t = F_{t-1}(x_t)\) should be i.i.d. \(U(0,1)\).

A failed diagnostic says the model is wrong; a passed one does not say it is right.

🔧 Tool 2: Sequential Testing

Shape the model by testing parameter restrictions.

(A) General-to-specific (GETS). Start from a large \(AR(\bar p)\) and test \(H_0: \phi_{\bar p} = 0\).

  • \(H_0\) rejected → keep \(AR(\bar p)\) and stop.
  • \(H_0\) not rejected → move to \(AR(\bar p - 1)\) and test \(\phi_{\bar p - 1} = 0\).

Only restrictions the data accept are imposed.

(B) Specific-to-general. Start small and relax restrictions that are rejected. It fails, in general: every test is run inside a model that is still misspecified, so its size and power are not what the tables say.

🔧 Tool 3: Information Criteria

Choose the model that minimises \[IC(k) = \underbrace{-2\log\hat{\mathcal L}_k}_{\text{quality of fit}} + \underbrace{f(k)}_{\text{complexity}},\] with \(k\) the number of parameters and \(f\) increasing.

Criterion Penalty \(f(k)\)
Akaike (AIC) \(2k\)
Schwarz / Bayesian (SIC, BIC) \(k \log T\)
Hannan–Quinn (HQ) \(2k \log\log T\)

For \(T \ge 8\), \(\log T > 2\): BIC punishes each parameter harder than AIC, and HQ lies between them for \(T > 16\).

✏️ Information Criteria for an AR(p)

With Gaussian errors, the maximised log-likelihood of an AR(\(p\)) on \(T\) observations is \[\log\hat{\mathcal L} = -\tfrac T2\log(2\pi) - \tfrac T2\log\hat\sigma_p^2 - \tfrac T2, \qquad \hat\sigma_p^2 = \tfrac1T\textstyle\sum_t \hat\varepsilon_t^2.\]

Dropping the constants, every criterion becomes \[IC(p) = T\log\hat\sigma_p^2 + f(p+1),\] where \(k = p+1\) counts the slopes and the intercept. Dividing by \(T\) gives the per-observation form \(\log\hat\sigma^2_p + 2k/T\) for AIC.

Adding a lag never raises \(\hat\sigma_p^2\). The penalty is the only thing that stops the search at a finite \(p\). Compare all orders on the same sample: lose the first \(\bar p\) observations for every \(p\).

🧮 Worked Example: Pick an Order

\(T = 100\) observations; residual variances from AR(\(p\)), \(p = 0, \dots, 3\). Penalty per parameter: AIC \(2\), BIC \(\log 100 = 4.61\).

\(p\) \(\hat\sigma_p^2\) \(100\log\hat\sigma_p^2\) AIC BIC
0 1.500 40.55 42.55 45.15
1 1.200 18.23 22.23 27.44
2 1.150 13.98 19.98 27.79
3 1.130 12.22 20.22 30.64

AIC chooses \(p = 2\) (19.98). BIC chooses \(p = 1\) (27.44 < 27.79): the second lag lowers \(T\log\hat\sigma^2\) by 4.25, more than AIC’s price of 2 but less than BIC’s 4.61.

✏️ Why AIC Overfits

Let the true order be \(p_0\), and let it be in the candidate set. Compare \(p_0\) with \(p_0 + 1\).

The gain in fit from the redundant lag is the likelihood-ratio statistic, \[T\log\big(\hat\sigma^2_{p_0}/\hat\sigma^2_{p_0+1}\big) \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_1 .\] The criterion adds the lag when that gain exceeds the extra penalty \(c_T = f(k+1) - f(k)\):

  • AIC: \(c_T = 2\), so \(\Pr(\chi^2_1 > 2) = 0.157\) for every \(T\);
  • BIC: \(c_T = \log T \to \infty\), so the probability tends to \(0\).

✏️ Why BIC Is Consistent

Underfitting. Drop a true lag and \(\hat\sigma^2\) converges to a strictly larger number, so \(T\log\hat\sigma^2\) rises by an amount of order \(T\). Any penalty with \(c_T/T \to 0\) is outweighed, and the short model loses.

Consistency

A criterion selects \(p_0\) with probability tending to one if its per-parameter penalty satisfies \(c_T \to \infty\) and \(c_T/T \to 0\), and the true model is among finitely many candidates.

BIC (\(\log T\)) and HQ (\(2\log\log T\)) satisfy both; HQ’s is the slowest-growing penalty that still does. AIC fails the first.

💻 AIC vs BIC as \(T\) Grows

DGP: AR(2) with \(\phi = (0.5, -0.3)\); candidates \(p = 0, \dots, 8\). BIC’s hit rate climbs to 1; AIC’s overfit share stays near 0.27 for every \(T\).

icdata = {
  // AR(2) sample from a seeded generator, then Durbin-Levinson for sigma^2_p
  let s = seed * 9301 + 49297;
  const u = () => { s = (s * 16807) % 2147483647; return s / 2147483647; };
  const z = () => Math.sqrt(-2 * Math.log(u())) * Math.cos(2 * Math.PI * u());
  const N = T + 200, x = new Array(N).fill(0);
  for (let t = 2; t < N; t++) x[t] = 0.5 * x[t-1] - 0.3 * x[t-2] + z();
  const y = x.slice(200), m = d3.mean(y), P = 10;
  const g = d3.range(P + 1).map(k => d3.sum(d3.range(T - k), t => (y[t]-m)*(y[t+k]-m)) / T);
  let phi = [], s2 = [g[0]];
  for (let k = 1; k <= P; k++) {
    const a = (g[k] - d3.sum(d3.range(k - 1), j => phi[j] * g[k-1-j])) / s2[k-1];
    phi = phi.map((p, j) => p - a * phi[k-2-j]).concat([a]);
    s2.push(s2[k-1] * (1 - a * a));
  }
  const pen = {AIC: 2, BIC: Math.log(T), HQ: 2 * Math.log(Math.log(T))};
  const out = [];
  for (const c of ["AIC", "BIC", "HQ"]) {
    const v = s2.map((v2, p) => T * Math.log(v2) + pen[c] * (p + 1));
    const mn = d3.min(v);
    v.forEach((vv, p) => out.push({p, crit: c, ic: Math.min(vv - mn, 30), best: vv === mn}));
  }
  return out;
}

🎛️ What the Curves Show

The true order is \(p_0 = 2\). Large dots mark each criterion’s choice.

  • To the left of \(p_0\) all three curves are steep, and the steepness grows with \(T\): underfitting costs order \(T\).
  • To the right of \(p_0\) AIC’s curve is nearly flat. A lucky lag can tip it, at any \(T\).
  • BIC’s right-hand side climbs by about \(\log T\) per lag, so at \(T = 2000\) it rises by 7.6 per lag and noise cannot overturn that.
  • At \(T = 30\), try a few samples: BIC often stops at \(p = 1\). Consistency is a large-sample property.

💰 Case Study: Three Criteria and GETS

📦 AR(0)–AR(12): criteria, GETS, Ljung–Box 
u <- read.csv("data/UNRATE.csv"); x <- diff(u$UNRATE)
pmax <- 12; X <- embed(x, pmax + 1); y <- X[, 1]; n <- length(y)
fits <- lapply(0:pmax, function(p) if (p == 0) lm(y ~ 1) else lm(y ~ X[, 1 + seq_len(p)]))
s2 <- sapply(fits, function(f) mean(resid(f)^2)); k <- 1:(pmax + 1)
ic <- data.frame(p = 0:pmax, AIC = n*log(s2) + 2*k, BIC = n*log(s2) + k*log(n),
                 HQ = n*log(s2) + 2*k*log(log(n)))
p_ic <- sapply(ic[, -1], which.min) - 1
p <- pmax                                 # GETS: drop the last lag while |t| < 1.96
while (p > 0 && abs(coef(summary(fits[[p + 1]]))[p + 1, 3]) < 1.96) p <- p - 1
lb <- sapply(c(p_ic, GETS = p), function(q) Box.test(resid(fits[[q + 1]]), lag = 24,
                                                       type = "Ljung", fitdf = q)$p.value)
data.frame(rule = names(lb), order = c(p_ic, p), LjungBox24_pval = signif(lb, 2), row.names = NULL)
  rule order LjungBox24_pval
1  AIC    12         0.00036
2  BIC     4         0.00014
3   HQ     5         0.00027
4 GETS    12         0.00036

The criteria disagree by eight lags; every residual series fails the Ljung–Box test at lag 24.

💰 Case Study: What the Data Say

What the data say: BIC stops at 4 lags, HQ at 5; AIC and GETS run to the boundary at 12. Even AR(12) fails Ljung–Box: its residuals keep a correlation of −0.18 at lag 24, a trace of seasonal adjustment. And unemployment rises fast and falls slowly, an asymmetry no linear AR captures (Lecture 11).

📋 Key Formulas

Conditional mean \(\mu_t = E_{t-1}[x_t]\), minimises \(E[(x_t - g)^2]\) over \(g \in I_{t-1}\)
Conditional variance \(\sigma_t^2 = E_{t-1}[(x_t - \mu_t)^2]\)
Conditional quantile \(\Pr_{t-1}\{x_t \le q_t^\alpha\} = \alpha\); median at \(\alpha = 0.5\)
Information criterion \(IC(k) = -2\log\hat{\mathcal L}_k + f(k)\) → min
AR(\(p\)), Gaussian \(IC(p) = T\log\hat\sigma_p^2 + f(p+1)\)
Penalties AIC \(2k\); BIC \(k\log T\); HQ \(2k\log\log T\)
Consistency penalty per parameter \(c_T \to \infty\), \(c_T/T \to 0\)

📋 Summary

  • Non-structural modelling begins by choosing an object: a feature of the distribution of \(x_t\) given its past.
  • The conditional mean is optimal under squared loss, the median under absolute loss, a quantile under check loss.
  • Selection runs object → class → model; diagnostics, GETS and information criteria are the tools.
  • BIC and HQ are consistent; AIC overfits with positive probability at every \(T\), which is not always a defect when the goal is prediction.
  • All of these criteria judge in-sample fit.
