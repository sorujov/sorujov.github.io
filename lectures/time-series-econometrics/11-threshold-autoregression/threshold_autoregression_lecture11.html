🗺️ Where We Are

Lecture 10 ended on a limitation: in a sample, a unit root and a root of \(0.999\) look the same. A unit-root test measures persistence, nothing more.

Some series are persistent, yet economic theory says they must revert: the real exchange rate under purchasing power parity is the standard case. A linear AR can only make such a series uniformly persistent. Today the dynamics are allowed to depend on the state of the series.

❓ The Question

Does unemployment rise faster than it falls?

In US recessions the unemployment rate jumps by several points within a year. In expansions it drifts down by a tenth of a point a month for years.

A linear AR for \(\Delta u_t\) has one set of coefficients, so a rise and a fall of the same size must be followed by mirror-image paths. If the dynamics differ between “unemployment rising” and “unemployment falling”, a linear model averages two different economies into one that exists in neither.

💡 Persistence Without a Unit Root

Nonlinear modelling can build a bridge between empirical properties and economic theory.

  • Empirically, real exchange rates are very persistent; unit-root tests often fail to reject.
  • In theory, PPP says the real exchange rate fluctuates around zero (its log deviation from parity).
  • A nonlinear model reconciles the two: little or no reversion near parity, strong reversion far from it.

The series is persistent, but it has no unit root. We return to this example with numbers below.

💡 Predictable White Noise

Let \(\varepsilon_t\) be strong white noise (i.i.d., mean 0, variance 1) and \[y_t=\varepsilon_t+\theta\,\varepsilon_{t-1}\varepsilon_{t-2}.\]

For \(j\neq0\), every term of \(E(y_ty_{t-j})\) contains a factor \(\varepsilon_s\) that appears exactly once, so it vanishes: \(\operatorname{cov}(y_t,y_{t-j})=0\). Thus \(y_t\) is white noise, though not independent.

The best linear forecast is therefore \(\hat y^{\,\text{lin}}_{t+1}=0\). The conditional mean is not: \[\hat y^{\,\text{nonlin}}_{t+1}=E(y_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t)=\theta\,\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t-1}.\] A nonlinear model can reveal information that no linear model can detect.

💻 White Noise You Can Forecast

📦 Simulate y_t = e_t + 0.8 e_{t-1} e_{t-2} 
n <- 5000; theta <- 0.8; e <- rnorm(n + 2)
y  <- e[3:(n + 2)] + theta * e[2:(n + 1)] * e[1:n]
round(acf(y, lag.max = 5, plot = FALSE)$acf[2:6], 3)     # autocorrelations, lags 1-5
f_nonlin <- theta * e[2:(n + 1)] * e[1:n]                # E(y_t | past)
c(R2_linear    = 0,
  R2_nonlinear = round(1 - mean((y - f_nonlin)^2) / var(y), 3),
  theory       = round(theta^2 / (1 + theta^2), 3))
[1] -0.011 -0.008 -0.001 -0.018 -0.012
   R2_linear R2_nonlinear       theory 
       0.000        0.372        0.390

Every autocorrelation is within \(\pm0.02\) of zero, yet the conditional mean explains over a third of the variance. A close relative is the bilinear model: an ARMA plus cross terms \(\sum_{i,j}\psi_{ij}x_{t-i}\varepsilon_{t-j}\).

📐 Threshold Autoregression (TAR)

Models with regimes are the nonlinear family most used in economics. In a TAR there are most often two regimes, with linear AR dynamics in each: \[x_t=\begin{cases}\mu_1+\rho_1x_{t-1} & \text{if } q_{t-1}\le\gamma\\[2pt] \mu_2+\rho_2x_{t-1} & \text{if } q_{t-1}>\gamma\end{cases}\;+\;\varepsilon_t, \qquad E_{t-1}\varepsilon_t=0,\ \operatorname{var}_{t-1}\varepsilon_t=\sigma^2.\]

  • The switch is driven by an event: the threshold variable \(q_{t-1}\) exceeds the threshold \(\gamma\).
  • \(q_{t-1}\) must be realised in the previous period, so the regime is known before \(x_t\) is drawn.
  • The switches are endogenous when \(q_{t-1}\) depends on the series itself.

📐 SETAR, and Choosing \(q_{t-1}\)

If \(q_{t-1}=x_{t-d}\) for a known or unknown delay \(d\), the model is a self-exciting TAR (SETAR). The choice of \(q_{t-1}\) depends on the frequency and nature of the data:

Series Threshold variable Regimes
GNP growth, quarterly \(q_{t-1}=y_{t-2}\) contraction / expansion
unemployment, monthly \(q_{t-1}=\Delta x_{t-d}\) or \(x_{t-1}-x_{t-d}\) rising / falling
nominal exchange rate, weekly returns \(q_{t-1}=\frac15\sum_{j=1}^{5}\lvert x_{t-j}\rvert\) high / low volatility
real exchange rate, quarterly \(q_{t-1}=\lvert x_{t-1}\rvert\) near / far from PPP

📐 The Real Exchange Rate Band

Let \(x_t\) be the log deviation of the real exchange rate from PPP, and \(q_{t-1}=|x_{t-1}|\):

  • inner regime, \(|x_{t-1}|\le\gamma\): estimates give \(\rho_1=1.06\), locally explosive;
  • outer regime, \(|x_{t-1}|>\gamma\): \(\rho_2\approx0.92\), mean-reverting.

Inside the band, transaction costs make arbitrage unprofitable, and the rate drifts away from parity. Outside it, arbitrage pulls it back. A unit-root test will typically find \(I(1)\), yet the outer regime forces the process back towards the band: it is globally stationary.

💻 Simulating the Band

Explosive inside the shaded band, \(\rho_1=1.06\); mean-reverting outside it, \(\rho_2=0.92\); \(\gamma=0.10\), \(\sigma=0.03\).

💻 The Band Fools the DF Test

📦 DF τ test (constant) on 2,000 band paths of 160 quarters 
dft <- function(x) { y <- x[-1]; X <- cbind(1, x[-length(x)]); f <- lm.fit(X, y)
  s2 <- sum(f$residuals^2) / (length(y) - 2)
  (f$coefficients[2] - 1) / sqrt(s2 * solve(crossprod(X))[2, 2]) }
c(rejection_rate_5pct = mean(replicate(2000, dft(sim_band(160)) < -2.86)))
rejection_rate_5pct 
             0.2065

The process is globally stationary, yet in forty years of quarterly data the DF test fails to reject the unit root about four times in five.

📐 Stationarity of a TAR

\(|\rho_1|<1\) and \(|\rho_2|<1\) is sufficient but not necessary.

  • One regime may have a unit root, or even \(\rho>1\), and the process can still be globally stationary.
  • What makes this possible is an endogenous switch: the threshold variable depends on \(x\) itself, so an explosive regime pushes the process out of that regime.

For SETAR(1) with \(q_{t-1}=x_{t-1}\) and zero intercepts, the process is ergodic if and only if \[\rho_1<1,\qquad \rho_2<1,\qquad \rho_1\rho_2<1\] (Petrucelli and Woolford, 1984). So \(\rho_1=-3\), \(\rho_2=0.2\) is stationary: \(|\rho_1|>1\) but \(\rho_1\rho_2=-0.6<1\).

🔧 Extensions: Lags and Regimes

Different lag orders. Regime \(j\) has its own AR(\(p_j\)): \[x_t=\mu_j+\rho_{j,1}x_{t-1}+\dots+\rho_{j,p_j}x_{t-p_j}+\varepsilon_t\quad\text{in regime } j .\] Choose \((p_1,p_2)\) by minimising \(IC_1+IC_2\), for example \[BIC_j=T_j\log\hat\sigma_j^2+p_j\log T_j,\] where \(T_j\) is the number of observations in regime \(j\).

Multiple regimes, same threshold variable, with \(\gamma_0<\gamma_1<\dots<\gamma_m\): \[x_t=\mu_j+\rho_jx_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\quad\text{if }\gamma_{j-1}\le q_{t-1}<\gamma_j,\qquad j=1,\dots,m.\]

🔧 Extensions: Two Thresholds

Four regimes, governed by \(x_{t-1}\) and \(x_{t-2}\) with thresholds \(\gamma_1,\gamma_2\): \[x_t=\begin{cases}\mu_1+\rho_1x_{t-1} & \text{if }x_{t-2}\le\gamma_2,\ x_{t-1}\le\gamma_1\\ \mu_2+\rho_2x_{t-1} & \text{if }x_{t-2}\le\gamma_2,\ x_{t-1}>\gamma_1\\ \mu_3+\rho_3x_{t-1} & \text{if }x_{t-2}>\gamma_2,\ x_{t-1}\le\gamma_1\\ \mu_4+\rho_4x_{t-1} & \text{if }x_{t-2}>\gamma_2,\ x_{t-1}>\gamma_1\end{cases}\;+\;\varepsilon_t .\]

Each added threshold multiplies the number of regimes, and divides the observations available to each.

🔧 Extensions: Rules and Variances

A general switching rule. Replace \(q_{t-1}\lessgtr\gamma\) by a condition on \[f(x_{t-1},\dots,x_{t-d},\vartheta),\] where the form of \(f\) is known a priori, the delay \(d\) may be unknown, and \(\vartheta\) is an unknown finite-dimensional parameter.

Regime-wise heteroskedasticity: \[\operatorname{var}_{t-1}\varepsilon_t=\begin{cases}\sigma_1^2 & \text{if }q_{t-1}\le\gamma\\ \sigma_2^2 & \text{if }q_{t-1}>\gamma\end{cases}\]

Conditional heteroskedasticity: \(\operatorname{var}_{t-1}\varepsilon_t\) not constant, for example GARCH-type errors (Lecture 15).

📐 Estimation: The Problem

Back to the basic model, written with indicators: \[x_t=(\mu_1+\rho_1x_{t-1})\,\mathbb 1\{q_{t-1}\le\gamma\}+(\mu_2+\rho_2x_{t-1})\,\mathbb 1\{q_{t-1}>\gamma\}+\varepsilon_t .\] Parameters: \(\theta=(\mu_1,\rho_1,\mu_2,\rho_2,\gamma,d)\), with \(d\) possibly, and \(\sigma^2\). Because \(\gamma\) is unknown, the model is nonlinear.

Gaussian QML is equivalent to NLLS: \[\min_\theta\sum_t\Big(x_t-(\mu_1+\rho_1x_{t-1})\mathbb 1\{q_{t-1}\le\gamma\}-(\mu_2+\rho_2x_{t-1})\mathbb 1\{q_{t-1}>\gamma\}\Big)^2 .\] With respect to \(\gamma\) the objective is not only non-differentiable but not even continuous. Gradient methods are useless.

🔧 The Concentration Method

Split the parameters into \(\gamma\) and the rest. For fixed \(\gamma\) the model is linear.

  1. Grid. Make a grid \(\Gamma\) for \(\gamma\). If \(q\) is a lag of \(x\), put the grid points at the observed values of \(q_{t-1}\). Trim the lowest and highest 10–15%: too few observations in a regime do not allow its parameters to be estimated.
  2. OLS for each \(\gamma\in\Gamma\). Regress \(x_t\) on the regime-split regressors and record \(\hat\sigma^2(\gamma)\).
  3. Minimise. \(\hat\gamma=\arg\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\hat\sigma^2(\gamma)\), and \(\hat\theta=\hat\theta(\hat\gamma)\), the OLS estimates at \(\hat\gamma\).

If \(d\) is unknown, minimise over the pairs \((d,\gamma)\).

✍️ Worked Example: The Data

Six observations, and a TAR with regime means only: \(x_t=\mu_1\mathbb 1\{q_{t-1}\le\gamma\}+\mu_2\mathbb 1\{q_{t-1}>\gamma\}+\varepsilon_t\).

\(q_{t-1}\) −1.0 −0.6 −0.2 0.1 0.4 0.9
\(x_t\) −0.5 −0.7 −0.3 0.6 0.4 0.8

Grid. Put candidate thresholds at the observed \(q\). Require at least two observations in each regime: this trims \(\gamma=-1.0\), \(0.4\) and \(0.9\), leaving \[\Gamma=\{-0.6,\ -0.2,\ 0.1\}.\] For each \(\gamma\) the OLS estimates are the two regime means, and \(SSR(\gamma)\) is the sum of squared deviations within regimes.

✍️ Worked Example: The Grid

\(\gamma\) regime 1 \(\hat\mu_1\) regime 2 \(\hat\mu_2\) \(SSR(\gamma)\)
−0.6 −0.5, −0.7 −0.600 −0.3, 0.6, 0.4, 0.8 0.375 0.020 + 0.688 = 0.708

So \(\hat\gamma=-0.2\), \(\hat\mu_1=-0.5\), \(\hat\mu_2=0.6\), \(\hat\sigma^2=0.160/6=0.027\).

Any \(\gamma\in[-0.2,\,0.1)\) splits the sample identically, so \(\hat\sigma^2(\gamma)\) is flat there: the objective is a step function, and only its values at the observed \(q\) matter.

💻 SETAR by Hand

📦 Simulate a SETAR(1), then estimate it by concentration 
set.seed(2028); n <- 300; x <- numeric(n + 1)
for (t in 2:(n + 1)) x[t] <- if (x[t - 1] <= 0) 0.4 + 0.6 * x[t - 1] + 0.5 * rnorm(1) else
                                               -0.2 + 0.3 * x[t - 1] + 0.5 * rnorm(1)
y <- x[-1]; q <- x[-(n + 1)]                          # q_{t-1} = x_{t-1}: self-exciting
setar_s2 <- function(g) { I <- q <= g; J <- !I              # regime indicators
  mean(lm.fit(cbind(I, I * q, J, J * q), y)$residuals^2) }
grid <- sort(q); grid <- grid[grid >= quantile(q, 0.15) & grid <= quantile(q, 0.85)]
s2   <- sapply(grid, setar_s2)
g_hat <- grid[which.min(s2)]; I <- q <= g_hat; J <- !I
b <- lm.fit(cbind(I, I * q, J, J * q), y)$coefficients
round(c(gamma_hat = g_hat, mu1 = b[[1]], rho1 = b[[2]], mu2 = b[[3]], rho2 = b[[4]],
        grid_points = length(grid)), 3)
  gamma_hat         mu1        rho1         mu2        rho2 grid_points 
      0.000       0.311       0.485      -0.122       0.296     210.000

True values: \(\gamma_0=0\), \((\mu_1,\rho_1)=(0.4,0.6)\), \((\mu_2,\rho_2)=(-0.2,0.3)\). The threshold is found almost exactly; the slopes carry ordinary sampling error of about \(\pm0.1\).

📊 The Objective Is a Step Function

Flat between neighbouring observations, jumping at each one; rough overall, with a sharp dip whose lowest step (red) is \(\hat\gamma\).

📐 Asymptotic Theory

Because of the discontinuity, the usual asymptotic theory does not apply. Yet the outcome is good news.

  • \(\hat\mu_1,\hat\rho_1,\hat\mu_2,\hat\rho_2,\hat\sigma^2\) are standard: consistent, \(\sqrt T\)-rate, asymptotically normal, with the same limit as if \(\gamma\) were known.
  • \(\hat\gamma\) is \(T\)-consistent: \(\hat\gamma-\gamma=O_p(T^{-1})\). Because it converges so fast, we may act as if \(\hat\gamma\) were the true value when doing inference on the slopes.
  • The discontinuity looks like a curse; it is in fact a blessing.

The catch: the limit of \(T(\hat\gamma-\gamma)\) is non-pivotal. It depends on unknown parameters and is very hard to standardise. How, then, do we build a confidence interval for \(\gamma\)? That question opens the next lecture.

🧠 Think–Pair–Share

  1. In the step plot, why is \(\hat\sigma^2(\gamma)\) constant between two neighbouring observed values of \(q_{t-1}\)?
  2. What goes wrong if we search \(\gamma\) all the way to the smallest observed \(q_{t-1}\)?
  3. A colleague estimates \(\gamma\) with a Newton–Raphson routine started at the median of \(q_{t-1}\). What will happen?

✅ Think–Pair–Share: Solution

  1. \(\hat\sigma^2(\gamma)\) depends on \(\gamma\) only through the split \(\{t: q_{t-1}\le\gamma\}\). Between two neighbouring observations the split does not change, so neither does the OLS fit.
  1. Regime 1 would hold one or two observations: its two parameters are barely or not at all estimable, and \(\hat\sigma^2(\gamma)\) can be driven down by fitting a handful of points exactly. Trimming 10–15% at each end prevents this.
  1. The derivative of a step function is zero wherever it exists. Newton–Raphson sees a flat objective and stops where it started. Grid search over observed values is exact, and cheap: one OLS per grid point.

du <- c(NA, diff(u)); tt <- 14:N                    # d = 12 needs u_{t-13}

💰 Case Study: What the Data Say

What the data say: \(\hat\gamma=0\). When unemployment is no higher than a year ago it drifts down by about 0.04 points a month; when it is higher, it rises with momentum (coefficient 0.37 on \(\Delta u_{t-2}\)). The model is silent on whether the gain over the linear AR(2) is more than chance: next lecture.

📝 Quiz #2: Stationarity

A SETAR(1) with \(q_{t-1}=x_{t-1}\) and zero intercepts has \(\rho_1=1.2\) in the lower regime and \(\rho_2=0.5\) in the upper. Is it stationary?

  • No: the condition \(\rho_1<1\) fails
  • Yes: \(\rho_1\rho_2=0.6<1\)
  • Yes: the upper regime pulls it back
  • Only if \(\sigma^2\) is small

📝 Quiz #3: Superconsistency

\(\hat\gamma\) is \(T\)-consistent. What does this buy us?

  • Inference on \(\mu_j,\rho_j\) can proceed as if \(\gamma\) were known
  • A normal confidence interval \(\hat\gamma\pm1.96\,SE(\hat\gamma)\)
  • The objective becomes differentiable in large samples
  • No trimming is needed

📋 Key Formulas

predictable white noise \(y_t=\varepsilon_t+\theta\varepsilon_{t-1}\varepsilon_{t-2}\), \(E(y_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t)=\theta\varepsilon_t\varepsilon_{t-1}\)
TAR \(x_t=(\mu_1+\rho_1x_{t-1})\mathbb 1\{q_{t-1}\le\gamma\}+(\mu_2+\rho_2x_{t-1})\mathbb 1\{q_{t-1}>\gamma\}+\varepsilon_t\)
SETAR \(q_{t-1}=x_{t-d}\)
SETAR(1) ergodic \(\rho_1<1,\ \rho_2<1,\ \rho_1\rho_2<1\)
lag orders \(\min_{p_1,p_2}(IC_1+IC_2)\), \(BIC_j=T_j\log\hat\sigma_j^2+p_j\log T_j\)
concentration \(\hat\gamma=\arg\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\hat\sigma^2(\gamma)\), \(\hat\theta=\hat\theta(\hat\gamma)\)
rates \(\hat\gamma-\gamma=O_p(T^{-1})\); slopes \(\sqrt T\)-normal

📋 Summary

  • Nonlinear models give persistence without a unit root, and forecasts where linear ones see white noise
  • A TAR switches between linear regimes when an observed \(q_{t-1}\) crosses \(\gamma\)
  • An endogenous switch can make a TAR stationary despite an explosive regime
  • Estimation: OLS for each \(\gamma\) on a trimmed grid of observed values; take the minimum
  • Slopes are \(\sqrt T\)-normal as if \(\gamma\) were known; \(\hat\gamma\) is \(T\)-consistent with a non-pivotal limit
