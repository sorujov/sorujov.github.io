🗺️ Where We Are

Lecture 9 ended on a limit: \[T(\hat\rho-1)\ \xrightarrow{d}\ \frac{\int_0^1 \mathcal B\,d\mathcal B}{\int_0^1 \mathcal B(r)^2dr}.\] It is non-normal, skewed to the left, and free of \(\sigma^2\).

Today that limit becomes a test. Three questions decide whether the test is usable in practice: which deterministic terms enter the regression, what to do when the errors are serially correlated, and what a rejection or non-rejection actually tells us.

❓ The Question

Are recessions forgotten?

US real GDP fell about 4% in 2008–09. Does log GDP return to its old trend line, or is the loss carried forward for ever?

If log GDP is trend-stationary, a shock dies out and the old path returns. If it has a unit root, every shock shifts the whole future path, and “returning to trend” has no meaning. The two views give the same short-run forecasts and very different long-run ones.

📐 Random-Walk Moments (FCLT)

Let \(\varepsilon_t\) be white noise with variance \(\sigma^2\) and \(x_t=x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\). With \(S_T(r)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt T}\,x_{[Tr]}\), \[S_T(r)\ \Rightarrow\ \mathcal B(r)\quad\text{(standard Brownian motion on }[0,1]),\] \[\frac1T\sum_{t=2}^{T}x_{t-1}\varepsilon_t\ \xrightarrow{d}\ \sigma^2\!\int_0^1\!\mathcal B\,d\mathcal B,\qquad \frac1{T^2}\sum_{t=2}^{T}x_{t-1}^2\ \xrightarrow{d}\ \sigma^2\!\int_0^1\!\mathcal B(r)^2dr,\] jointly.

  • Remark 1. The same holds for weak white noise that is a martingale difference sequence; the limit still has independent increments.
  • Remark 2. It holds for \(x_0\neq0\), even random, provided \(x_0\) is bounded in probability.

📐 OLS Under a Unit Root

Model \(x_t=\rho x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\) with \(\rho=1\). The OLS estimator is \[\hat\rho=\frac{\sum_{t=2}^{T}x_tx_{t-1}}{\sum_{t=2}^{T}x_{t-1}^2}=1+\frac{\sum_{t=2}^{T}x_{t-1}\varepsilon_t}{\sum_{t=2}^{T}x_{t-1}^2}.\]

Scale the numerator by \(T\) and the denominator by \(T^2\): \[T(\hat\rho-1)=\frac{\frac1T\sum x_{t-1}\varepsilon_t}{\frac1{T^2}\sum x_{t-1}^2}\ \xrightarrow{d}\ \underbrace{\frac{\int_0^1\mathcal B\,d\mathcal B}{\int_0^1\mathcal B(r)^2\,dr}}_{DF\ \text{distribution}}.\] The \(\sigma^2\) cancels. The rate is \(T\), not \(\sqrt T\): \(\hat\rho\) is superconsistent.

📐 Pivotal, and Skewed to the Left

Compare the stationary case, \(|\rho|<1\): \[\sqrt T(\hat\rho-\rho)\ \xrightarrow{d}\ N(0,\,1-\rho^2).\] This limit depends on \(\rho\): it is non-pivotal, and we must standardise before tabulating.

The DF distribution contains no unknown parameter: it is pivotal, and can be tabulated once, by simulation.

Its shape: the numerator is \(\int_0^1\mathcal B\,d\mathcal B=\tfrac12\{\mathcal B(1)^2-1\}\), and the denominator is positive, so \[P\big(T(\hat\rho-1)<0\big)\to P\big(\chi^2(1)<1\big)\approx0.68.\] Two thirds of the mass lies below zero: \(\hat\rho\) is biased downwards.

📐 The Dickey–Fuller Test

\[H_0:\ \rho=1\ \text{(unit root)}\qquad\text{vs}\qquad H_A:\ \rho<1\ \text{(stationarity)}\]

The test is one-sided and left-tailed. Two statistics:

  • the coefficient statistic: reject \(H_0\) if \(T(\hat\rho-1)<q_\alpha^{DF}\);
  • the \(t\)-ratio, \[\tau=\frac{\hat\rho-1}{SE(\hat\rho)}\ \xrightarrow{d}\ \frac{\int_0^1\mathcal B\,d\mathcal B}{\big(\int_0^1\mathcal B(r)^2dr\big)^{1/2}}=DF_\tau ,\] and reject if \(\tau<q_\alpha^{DF_\tau}\).

\(\tau\) is the ordinary OLS \(t\)-statistic. Only its reference distribution changes: \(DF_\tau\), not \(N(0,1)\).

💻 Simulating the DF Distributions

df_stats <- function(x, det) {             # returns T(rho_hat - 1) and tau
  y <- x[-1]; xl <- x[-length(x)]; n <- length(y)
  X <- switch(det, none  = cbind(xl), const = cbind(1, xl),
                   trend = cbind(1, seq_len(n), xl))
  fit <- .lm.fit(X, y); k <- ncol(X); b <- fit$coefficients[k]
  se  <- sqrt(sum(fit$residuals^2) / (n - k) * solve(crossprod(X))[k, k])
  c(n * (b - 1), (b - 1) / se)
}

📊 DF Is Not N(0,1)

Dashed: \(N(0,1)\); dotted: its 5% point, \(-1.645\). Each DF density lies left of the normal, further left with each deterministic term.

📐 Adding a Constant

Regress \(x_t=\mu+\rho x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\) by OLS, obtaining \((\hat\mu,\hat\rho)\). Under \(\rho=1\), \[T(\hat\rho-1)\ \xrightarrow{d}\ \frac{\int_0^1\bar{\mathcal B}(r)\,d\bar{\mathcal B}(r)}{\int_0^1\bar{\mathcal B}(r)^2\,dr}=\overline{DF}, \qquad \bar{\mathcal B}(r)=\mathcal B(r)-\int_0^1\mathcal B(s)\,ds .\]

\(\bar{\mathcal B}\) is the demeaned Brownian motion: estimating \(\mu\) is the continuous-time analogue of subtracting the sample mean from \(x_{t-1}\).

The limit depends on which deterministic terms are in the regression, never on their true values. It is again pivotal.

📐 Adding a Trend

Regress \(x_t=\mu+\delta t+\rho x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\), obtaining \((\hat\mu,\hat\delta,\hat\rho)\). Under \(\rho=1\), \[T(\hat\rho-1)\ \xrightarrow{d}\ \frac{\int_0^1\tilde{\mathcal B}(r)\,d\tilde{\mathcal B}(r)}{\int_0^1\tilde{\mathcal B}(r)^2\,dr}=\widetilde{DF}, \qquad \tilde{\mathcal B}(r)=\mathcal B(r)-b_0-b_1r,\] \[\begin{pmatrix}b_0\\ b_1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\int_0^1(4-6r)\,\mathcal B(r)\,dr\\[2pt] \int_0^1(12r-6)\,\mathcal B(r)\,dr\end{pmatrix}.\]

\(b_0+b_1r\) is the least-squares projection of \(\mathcal B\) on \(\{1,r\}\) in \(L^2[0,1]\): \(\tilde{\mathcal B}\) is \(\mathcal B\) with its own best-fitting line removed.

🔧 Which Deterministic Terms?

  • Always include a constant, unless the mean has already been removed from the series.
  • A trend depends on the situation: include it when the series trends, so that the stationary alternative is “stationary around a line”.
Do not include a time trend Include a time trend
inflation, unemployment, interest rates, exchange rates log GNP, log industrial production, log prices

Leaving a trend out of a trending series biases the test towards the unit root. Adding one needlessly costs power.

✍️ Worked Example: A DF Regression

Quarterly log real GDP, \(T=200\). With a constant and a trend, OLS gives \(\hat\rho=0.9620\), \(SE(\hat\rho)=0.0175\).

Step 1. Coefficient statistic: \(T(\hat\rho-1)=200\times(-0.0380)=-7.6\).

Step 2. \(t\)-ratio: \(\tau=-0.0380/0.0175=-2.17\).

Step 3. Compare with the constant + trend row: \(-7.6>-21.8\) and \(-2.17>-3.41\). Do not reject the unit root at 5%.

The trap. Against \(N(0,1)\), \(\tau=-2.17<-1.645\) would “reject”. The normal table overstates the evidence against a unit root.

📐 Serially Correlated Errors

Return to \(x_t=\rho x_{t-1}+u_t\), \(\rho=1\). If the true process is not a random walk, \(u_t=\Delta x_t\) is serially correlated. Write \[\Gamma_j=E(u_tu_{t-j}),\qquad \omega^2=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\Gamma_j,\qquad \lambda=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_j=\frac{\omega^2-\Gamma_0}{2}.\]

With \(\mathcal B\) now the Brownian motion from the FCLT normalised by \(\omega\), \[T(\hat\rho-1)\ \xrightarrow{d}\ \frac{\int_0^1\mathcal B\,d\mathcal B+\lambda/\omega^2}{\int_0^1\mathcal B(r)^2\,dr}.\] The extra term depends on nuisance parameters, so the DF tables no longer apply.

📐 Two Remedies

Nonparametric: Phillips–Perron. Subtract the bias directly: \[Z=T(\hat\rho-1)-\frac{\hat\lambda}{\frac1{T^2}\sum x_{t-1}^2}\ \xrightarrow{d}\ DF .\] \(\hat\lambda=(\hat\omega^2-\hat\Gamma_0)/2\) uses a Newey–West estimate \(\hat\omega^2\). A one-sided sum need not be positive, so no positive-definite estimate of \(\lambda\) is required.

Parametric: augmented Dickey–Fuller. Add lagged differences: \[x_t=\rho x_{t-1}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_j\,\underbrace{\Delta x_{t-j}}_{\text{stationary}}+\underbrace{\varepsilon_t}_{\text{pre-whitened}} .\] Choose \(p\) by BIC. Then \(ADF=\dfrac{T(\hat\rho-1)}{1-\sum_j\hat\alpha_j}\xrightarrow{d}DF\), and the \(t\)-ratio on \(\rho\) \(\xrightarrow{d}DF_\tau\).

💻 Does the Correction Work?

📦 Random walk with MA(1) increments: rejection rates at 5% 
nw_lrv <- function(u, L = floor(4 * (length(u) / 100)^(2 / 9))) {
  n <- length(u); g <- sapply(0:L, function(j) sum(u[(j + 1):n] * u[1:(n - j)]) / n)
  g[1] + 2 * sum((1 - (1:L) / (L + 1)) * g[-1])            # Bartlett kernel
}
stats3 <- function(x, pmax = 8) {          # all with a constant
  y <- x[-1]; xl <- x[-length(x)]; n <- length(y)
  f <- .lm.fit(cbind(1, xl), y); dfz <- n * (f$coefficients[2] - 1)
  lam <- (nw_lrv(f$residuals) - mean(f$residuals^2)) / 2
  pp <- dfz - lam / (sum((xl - mean(xl))^2) / n^2)
  dx <- diff(x); fitp <- function(p, i) { X <- cbind(x[i - 1], 1)
    if (p > 0) for (j in 1:p) X <- cbind(X, dx[i - 1 - j]); .lm.fit(X, x[i]) }
  i0 <- (pmax + 2):length(x)
  bic <- sapply(0:pmax, function(p) length(i0) * log(mean(fitp(p, i0)$residuals^2)) + (p + 2) * log(length(i0)))
  p <- which.min(bic) - 1; i <- (p + 2):length(x); b <- fitp(p, i)$coefficients
  adf <- length(i) * (b[1] - 1) / (1 - if (p > 0) sum(b[3:(p + 2)]) else 0)
  c(DF = dfz, PP = pp, ADF = adf)
}
rate <- function(theta, R = 2000, T = 200) { r <- t(replicate(R, {
  e <- rnorm(T + 1); stats3(cumsum(e[-1] + theta * e[-(T + 1)])) }))
  round(colMeans(r < -14.1), 3) }               # -14.1 = 5% point of DF-bar
rbind(`theta = -0.5` = rate(-0.5), `theta = 0` = rate(0), `theta = +0.5` = rate(0.5))

🧠 Think–Pair–Share

Two processes, driven by the same shocks, \(T=300\): \[\text{(A)}\ x_t=x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t,\qquad \text{(B)}\ x_t=0.999\,x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t .\]

  1. After a unit shock, how much remains in (B) after 10 periods? After 700?
  2. Would a DF test at 5% tell (A) and (B) apart? Guess the rejection rate under (B).
  3. Which of the two do you want for a 4-quarter GDP forecast? For a 50-year pension forecast?

✅ Think–Pair–Share: Solution

  1. In (B) the impulse response is \(0.999^h\): \(0.999^{10}=0.990\), \(0.999^{700}=0.496\). The half-life is \(\ln 0.5/\ln 0.999\approx693\) periods. In (A) the whole shock remains for ever.
  1. No. With \(T=300\) the two are practically indistinguishable; the rejection rate under (B) is barely above the 5% size (next slide).
  1. For four quarters it does not matter: short-run forecasts coincide. For fifty years it matters entirely: (B) reverts to its mean, (A) never does.

📈 The Twins: ρ = 1 and ρ = 0.999

Same shocks. In the sample the two paths are one line; only the forecasts part company.

📊 Can a Test Tell Them Apart?

📦 DF τ test (constant), 5%: rejection rates, T = 300 
rej <- function(rho, R = 2000) mean(replicate(R, {
  x <- as.numeric(stats::filter(rnorm(300), rho, method = "recursive"))
  df_stats(x, "const")[2] < -2.86 }))
c(`rho = 1` = rej(1), `rho = 0.999` = rej(0.999), `rho = 0.95` = rej(0.95))
    rho = 1 rho = 0.999  rho = 0.95 
      0.052       0.053       0.601

At \(\rho=0.999\) the test rejects about as often as under the null: its power is essentially its size. Even \(\rho=0.95\), a half-life of 14 periods, is missed four times in ten.

📐 What a Unit-Root Test Means

  • The test is about the persistence of shocks. Under a unit root, as for log GNP on this view, a shock lasts for ever.
  • In a finite sample, \(x_t=x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\) and \(x_t=0.999x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t\) cannot be told apart. Their short-run predictions are the same.
  • They differ only at long horizons, where the stationary forecast returns to the mean.
  • Low power against near-unit-root alternatives is therefore not a defect of a particular test: it is in the problem.
  • “Do not reject” means “the data are consistent with very persistent shocks”, not “\(\rho\) is exactly 1”.

📦 ADF regression with BIC lag choice, on vendored FRED data 
rd <- function(f) { d <- read.csv(f); d[[2]] <- suppressWarnings(as.numeric(d[[2]]))
  d <- na.omit(d); d$date <- as.Date(d$observation_date); d }
gdp <- rd("data/GDPC1.csv"); un <- rd("data/UNRATE.csv"); eu <- rd("data/DEXUSEU.csv")
eum <- aggregate(eu$DEXUSEU, list(m = format(eu$date, "%Y-%m")), mean)
adf <- function(x, det, pmax = 12) {
  dx <- diff(x); fitp <- function(p, i) {
    X <- cbind(x[i - 1], 1); if (det == "trend") X <- cbind(X, i)
    if (p > 0) for (j in 1:p) X <- cbind(X, dx[i - 1 - j])
    f <- lm.fit(X, x[i]); s2 <- sum(f$residuals^2) / (length(i) - ncol(X))
    list(f = f, k = ncol(X), se = sqrt(s2 * solve(crossprod(X))[1, 1])) }
  i0 <- (pmax + 2):length(x)
  bic <- sapply(0:pmax, function(p) { r <- fitp(p, i0)
    length(i0) * log(mean(r$f$residuals^2)) + r$k * log(length(i0)) })
  p <- which.min(bic) - 1; r <- fitp(p, (p + 2):length(x)); b <- r$f$coefficients[1]
  c(p = p, T = length(x) - p - 1, rho = round(b, 4), tau = round((b - 1) / r$se, 2),
    crit_5pct = if (det == "trend") -3.41 else -2.86)
}
rbind(log_GDP = adf(log(gdp$GDPC1), "trend"), unemployment = adf(un$UNRATE, "const"),
      log_EURUSD = adf(log(eum$x), "const"))
             p   T    rho   tau crit_5pct
log_GDP      0 317 0.9899 -1.25     -3.41
unemployment 5 858 0.9830 -4.17     -2.86

💰 Case Study: What the Data Say

What the data say: log GDP (\(\tau=-1.25\)) and the euro (\(\tau=-2.08\)) do not reject the unit root: GDP stayed below its pre-2008 trend. Unemployment rejects (\(\tau=-4.17\)): very persistent, yet mean-reverting. A non-rejection is equally consistent with \(\rho=0.99\).

📝 Quiz #1: Critical Values

A DF regression with a constant (no trend) gives \(\tau=-2.40\). At the 5% level you should:

  • Not reject the unit root, since \(-2.40>-2.86\)
  • Reject the unit root, since \(-2.40<-1.645\)
  • Reject the unit root, since \(|-2.40|>1.96\)
  • Not reject, since \(\tau\) must be positive to reject

📝 Quiz #2: Why ADF Adds Lags

In the augmented DF regression \(x_t=\rho x_{t-1}+\sum_{j=1}^p\alpha_j\Delta x_{t-j}+\varepsilon_t\), what is the role of the lagged differences?

  • They absorb the serial correlation in \(\Delta x_t\), so that \(\varepsilon_t\) is serially uncorrelated and the DF tables apply
  • They remove the deterministic trend
  • They make \(x_t\) stationary under the null
  • They raise the power against \(\rho=0.999\)

📝 Quiz #3: Pivotal

Why can the DF distribution be tabulated once and for all, while the stationary limit \(N(0,1-\rho^2)\) cannot?

  • The DF limit contains no unknown parameter; the stationary one depends on \(\rho\)
  • The DF limit is normal
  • The DF limit does not depend on the deterministic terms
  • The stationary limit has infinite variance

📋 Key Formulas

DF coefficient \(T(\hat\rho-1)\xrightarrow{d}\int_0^1\mathcal B\,d\mathcal B\big/\int_0^1\mathcal B^2dr\)
DF \(t\)-ratio \(\tau\xrightarrow{d}\int_0^1\mathcal B\,d\mathcal B\big/\big(\int_0^1\mathcal B^2dr\big)^{1/2}\)
constant / trend replace \(\mathcal B\) by \(\bar{\mathcal B}=\mathcal B-\int\mathcal B\), or \(\tilde{\mathcal B}=\mathcal B-b_0-b_1r\)
5% points, \(\tau\) \(-1.94\), \(-2.86\), \(-3.41\)
correlated errors limit gains \(+\lambda/\omega^2\) in the numerator, \(\lambda=\sum_{j\ge1}\Gamma_j\)
Phillips–Perron \(Z=T(\hat\rho-1)-\hat\lambda\big/\big(T^{-2}\sum x_{t-1}^2\big)\)
ADF \(T(\hat\rho-1)/(1-\sum_j\hat\alpha_j)\), \(p\) by BIC

📋 Summary

  • Under a unit root \(\hat\rho\) converges at rate \(T\) to a pivotal, left-skewed DF distribution
  • The test is left-tailed; the critical values come from DF tables, never from \(N(0,1)\)
  • Always include a constant; include a trend for trending series
  • Serial correlation adds a nuisance term: remove it with Phillips–Perron or with ADF lags
  • A unit root and \(\rho=0.999\) are indistinguishable in a sample and differ only in the long run
